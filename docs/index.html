Primer

Five facts needed to read the rest

This section matters because both tickets are about the same number, the height of the bucket tip, and the guide only makes sense once it is clear how the system arrives at it.

  1. A machine control system shows the operator, on a tablet in the cab, where the bucket's cutting edge is against the design the site is being dug to.
  2. It finds the bucket tip in two parts: a GNSS antenna and receiver give the position of the antenna, and angle sensors on the boom, stick, bucket and machine body give the geometry from the antenna to the tip (Excavator manual, p. 11).
  3. Satellite positioning alone is good to about a metre. RTK, real-time kinematic positioning, adds corrections from a base station and brings it to centimetres (general GNSS practice). Makin takes the corrections by UHF radio from a local base or over the internet by NTRIP (p. 152).
  4. So a wrong bucket height has two families of cause: the position, which is ticket two, or the geometry and its calibration, which is ticket one. Most checks in this guide exist to tell the two apart cheaply.
  5. Installation and main calibration are done by Makin approved technicians (p. 9). A support engineer works between the operator, the distributor's technician and Makin.

Terms

RTKReal-time kinematic positioning: satellite positions corrected with data from a base station, accurate to centimetres. General GNSS term.
FixThe receiver has resolved its RTK solution and the position is good to centimetres. General GNSS term; Makin's Survey manual speaks of an RTK fix position (p. 158).
FloatCorrections arrive but the receiver has not resolved the solution yet; the position is good to decimetres. General GNSS term.
NTRIPCorrections streamed over the internet. A Makin profile holds server, port, username, password and mountpoint (Excavator manual, p. 159).
MountpointThe stream on an NTRIP server; it decides which corrections the receiver gets (p. 159).
UHF baseA local base station on or near the site that sends corrections by radio (p. 152).
DOPDilution of precision: how much the satellites' geometry multiplies the errors. Lower is better; a calibration check wants it close to 1.0 or below (pp. 143 and 172).
hRMS, vRMSThe current horizontal and vertical accuracy, defined at the GNSS antenna and not at the bucket (pp. 13 and 143).
Known pointA surveyed point on the site with known coordinates, also called a benchmark or a fix point (pp. 63 and 172).
Tool pointThe point on the bucket the system reports: the centre or a corner of the cutting edge (p. 163).
Project offsetA height shift of up to 50 cm that matches the GNSS height to a known point, set only on a correctly calibrated machine (p. 72).
MultipathSatellite signals that reach the antenna after bouncing off buildings or terrain, which degrades the position (p. 187).
IB-1Makin's interface box: it powers the sensors and carries their data to the tablet (pp. 11 and 195).
DistributorThe dealer who sells, installs and supports Makin systems in its country, and the first line a support engineer serves.

This guide is written the same way.

Is the bucket within tolerance on the known point?

This matters because "off grade" becomes a decision only once it is a number against a tolerance. The calculator follows Makin's calibration check (Excavator manual, pp. 172 to 173): the open and the closed bucket on the same known point and, with tilt, the bucket at full tilt both ways.

The manual gives no number for the tolerance: it says a reading "within a few centimetres of the target" means the calibration is correct (p. 173), and the wheel loader manual says "within the project's tolerances" (p. 163 of that manual). The 3 cm filled in here is my placeholder; enter the project's tolerance instead. The pattern advice under the verdict is my rule of thumb, not Makin's procedure: I call the open and closed readings in agreement when they differ from each other by no more than the tolerance. If the site's fix points are loaded in the project, the tablet's own Fix point control already reports the horizontal and vertical difference for both bucket positions (pp. 63 to 67), and this calculator is for readings phoned or messaged in from any other known point. Next entries

What I would add to the knowledge base next

These are suggestions from reading the public material only. Makin's own knowledge base and partner portal sit behind a login, so they may already cover them.

A logged point as the first attachment of every positioning ticket

A point logged on the tablet records HRMS, VRMS, PDOP, the number of satellites, the NTRIP mountpoint, any project offset and the tool point that was used (Excavator manual, pp. 143 to 144). If distributors logged one point on the known point and attached its record to the ticket, the first reply could start from those values instead of from questions.

A symptom index over the release notes

The release notes already describe faults in the words a technician hears on the phone, such as a Tersus receiver that "lost fix status without notifying the user" (version 2.33.5) or "MiC4 tilt not working" (version 2.33.7). If each symptom pointed to the version that fixed it, a technician could compare it with the Version line in Status during the first call.

A calibration check card for machines with tilt

The manual asks for the check at full tilt and at different rotation angles on both sides (p. 173). A printed card with one row per bucket position and angle would let an operator write the readings down at the machine and send a photo of it with the ticket.

Limits

What this guide does not know

  • It is written from the public manuals alone. I have not used a Makin system or seen a Makin support case. Every Makin step comes from the user guides and release notes listed below.
  • The order is by cost alone. I do not know which causes are most common at Makin. With the ticket history, I would sort by how often a cause occurs and what its check costs.
  • It stops at "recalibrate". The bucket calibration procedure is a Makin' Learning course behind a login (Excavator manual, p. 171), so this guide does not describe it.
  • The tolerance is an input. The manual says "within a few centimetres" (p. 173) and gives no number. The 3 cm in the calculator is my placeholder.
  • Fix and float are general GNSS terms. The excavator manual does not define them, so the definitions here are general practice.
  • Hardware differs between installations. Receivers, antennas, cables and bases vary, so the hardware steps are generic.
  • Escalation here means what I would send. Makin's internal escalation rules are not public.
How it was built

Sources and checks

I downloaded the manuals and release notes from Makin's own download page and read the chapters on calibration, GNSS, correction sources, status and the appendix. Each step carries the pages it rests on. The short quotations are checked by a script in the repository, tools/check_quotes.py, against the page they cite in Makin's PDF; on 16 October 2026 it found every one where the page says it is.

The page is plain HTML and JavaScript. The counts at the top are computed in your browser from the list of steps the page ships, and the calculator runs in your browser and sends nothing anywhere.

I built the page with help from Claude, an AI model, for the code and the checks; the choice of tickets, the order of the checks and every judgement on the page are mine.
